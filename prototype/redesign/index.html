The Count of Monte Cristo
Chapter 3: The Catalans

Dantès was a young man of nineteen or twenty, of a tall stature, with fine black eyes and hair as black as a raven's wing. He had, in addition, that calm resolution peculiar to men accustomed from their childhood to wrestle with danger. The old man looked at him with something like pity.

"Well, then," said Caderousse, "you do not trust me, and yet you are sitting in my house." Dantès smiled, and the smile was neither kind nor cruel, only patient, as though he had all the time in the world to wait for the truth. He set the cup down and looked toward the harbour, where the lamps were being lit one by one.

At that moment the door opened, and a girl came in, carrying a basket of fish still wet from the market. She stopped when she saw the two men, and the colour rose in her face. "Mercédès," said Dantès, and nothing more was needed.

The evening went on in this way, with talk of ships and of weather, of the price of cloth in Marseilles, and of the wedding that would take place in the spring. Nobody spoke of the debt, though everyone in the room knew of it.
